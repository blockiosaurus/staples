# Market Brief

A market brief tells a busy business owner what changed in their market this week and what it means for them. The bar: they can read it in the time stated, and they know whether they need to act.

## When to use

- The weekly brief routine fires.
- The person asks "what's going on in my market?" or "what are competitors doing?".

## When not to use

- A single, specific research question. Answer that directly in a `report` document instead.
- Breaking news that cannot wait a week. Create a short task for the person instead (see "Urgent findings").

## Procedure

1. **Set the scope.** Read the Company Brief and the focus the person gave at setup. Write down, for yourself, three to six things to check: named competitors, customer groups, the product category, suppliers, rules that apply to the business, the local area if the business is local.
2. **Look back at the last brief.** Find your previous `brief` document. Do not repeat items unless something new happened. Follow up on anything you said was "worth watching".
3. **Gather.** Search for news, announcements, pricing pages, job posts, reviews, forum threads and official notices from the last 7 days (14 days for the first brief). Follow the `research-sources` skill for what counts as a good source.
4. **Filter.** For each finding, ask: "Would the owner do anything differently if they knew this?" Keep only the findings where the answer is yes or maybe. Drop general industry news that does not touch this business.
5. **Rank.** Put the finding with the biggest possible effect first. Effect beats novelty.
6. **Write** using the structure below.
7. **Check.** Every item has a source link and a date. Every "so what" is specific to this business. Remove any sentence that would fit any company.
8. **Save** the brief as the task document `brief`, comment with a two to four sentence summary and a link, and mark the task done.

## Structure

```md
# Market brief — <week of DATE>

## The short version
<Two or three sentences: the most important change and what you recommend.>

## What changed
### 1. <Headline in plain words>
What happened: <one or two sentences, with source link and date>
Why it matters to you: <one or two sentences, specific to this business>
What you could do: <one concrete option, or "nothing yet — worth watching">

### 2. ...

## Worth watching
- <Early signal, one line, with source>

## Questions this raises
- <Optional: a question worth researching next, with your recommendation>

## Sources
- <Title — publisher — date — link>
```

## Depth

- **Brief:** three to five items in "What changed", at most 400 words in total, a five-minute read. Leave out "Questions this raises" unless something is important.
- **Deep dive:** five to eight items, up to 1,200 words. Add background for each item, compare with what you reported before, and include "Questions this raises".

## When nothing much happened

Say so in one line ("A quiet week: nothing that changes what you should do."), then list two or three "Worth watching" items. A short honest brief is better than padding.

## Urgent findings

If you find something that cannot wait for the next brief (a direct competitor launches a rival offer, a price drop that undercuts them, a rule change with a near deadline), create a task for the person straight away with the headline, the source and your recommended next step. Mention it again in the next brief.

## Writing rules

- Lead with the owner, not the news: "Your main competitor cut prices by 15%" beats "Industry pricing update".
- Numbers beat adjectives. Give the number and where it came from.
- Use the Company Brief's words for products, customers and the market.
- No filler openings ("In today's fast-moving landscape...").
- Mark uncertainty plainly: "unconfirmed", "one source only", "rumour".
